# Advanced Analytica grammar and punctuation guide

Sets practical house conventions for clear, consistent British English in support of Creative, Assured and Practical writing. Intentional fragments are welcome when they sharpen meaning. Grammar choices should help readers understand the message.

Status: **DRAFT** · Version 1.0.0 · Updated 2026-11-11

This guide is bundled with [the grammar standard](grammar_and_punctuation_policy.json) and shared definitions in [the TOV policy](governance_policy.json). Refresh this skill package when its source policies change.

## How it supports our voice

- **Creative:** Allow purposeful fragments, rhythm and expressive headings while keeping meaning clear.
- **Assured:** Use precise punctuation, accurate figures, faithful quotations and consistent terminology.
- **Practical:** Make instructions, lists, dates, units and references easy to understand and use.

## Scope and exceptions

Authored Advanced Analytica prose, headings, lists and labels across websites, proposals, reports, presentations and internal communications.

- Faithful quotations and required legal or source wording retain their original form.
- Code, identifiers, URLs and machine-readable data keep the syntax their function requires.
- Labelled examples of wording to revise are excluded from whole-document style counts.

Do not turn a house preference into a claim that all other usage is grammatically incorrect. Deliberate fragments and justified stylistic choices can pass when they improve understanding.

Apply these priorities in order:

1. Preserve accuracy, meaning, official names and required legal or source wording.
2. Apply a recorded client or publication convention to the specified deliverable.
3. Apply each canonical rule in this standard or the TOV policy within its stated scope.
4. Use editorial judgement for contextual suggestions and record exceptions where needed.

## 1. British English and spelling

Rule: `aa.grammar.spelling.01`

- Use British English house spellings: organise, analyse, organisation, colour and centre.
- Use licence and practice as nouns; use license and practise as verbs. Use programme for general initiatives and program for software.
- Keep spelling consistent within authored prose. Preserve official names, faithful quotations, code and exact source text.

**Use:**

> We analyse how the programme works.

**Revise:**

> We analyze how the program works.

Use the house spellings for a general business initiative.

Exceptions:

- A specified client locale can override the spelling default for that deliverable; record it and apply it consistently.

## 2. Headings and capitalisation

Rule: `aa.grammar.headings.01`

- Use sentence case for headings: capitalise the first word, proper names and established acronyms.
- Omit the final full stop in a heading. Keep internal full stops when a heading contains two short statements. Use a question mark for a real question.
- Purposeful fragments are allowed. Make the heading informative and let the following text add useful detail.

**Use:**

> A clear scope. A tested build

**Revise:**

> A Clear Scope. A Tested Build.

Use sentence case and omit the final full stop.

Exceptions:

- Preserve the official capitalisation of product names, document titles being cited and exact interface labels.

## 3. Sentence construction and intentional fragments

Rule: `aa.grammar.sentences.01`

- Use complete sentences where the reader needs a full explanation. Keep subjects, verbs and references easy to follow.
- Intentional fragments are welcome when they sharpen meaning, establish rhythm or label a clear action. Use them purposefully in headlines, short copy and lists.
- Avoid accidental fragments, comma splices and strings of short statements that obscure the relationship between ideas.
- Follow the shared TOV guidance on active voice and sentence length.

**Use:**

> A clear scope. A tested build. A team ready to use it.

**Revise:**

> Because the workflow. Which the team.

Purposeful fragments communicate an idea; incomplete clauses here obscure it.

## 4. Commas and the serial comma

Rule: `aa.grammar.commas.01`

- Use commas to separate list items, introductory material and non-essential clauses when they help the reader follow the sentence.
- Omit the serial comma in a simple list. Add it before the final and or or when it removes ambiguity or separates complex items.
- Do not join two complete sentences with a comma alone. Use a full stop, a suitable conjunction or a semicolon.

**Use:**

> The scope is clear. The owner is missing.

**Revise:**

> The scope is clear, the owner is missing.

Separate the complete sentences.

Exceptions:

- A client or publication can require a consistent serial-comma convention; record that override.

## 5. Colons and semicolons

Rule: `aa.grammar.colons_semicolons.01`

- Use a colon after a clear lead-in to introduce an explanation, example or list.
- Use a semicolon to connect closely related complete sentences or separate complex list items. Prefer a full stop when that is easier to read.
- Do not use repeated colons or semicolons to carry a sentence through several changes of thought.

**Use:**

> Check three things: scope, ownership and access.

**Revise:**

> The things to check are: scope, ownership and access.

Make the lead-in complete rather than separating the verb from its list.

## 6. Apostrophes and plurals

Rule: `aa.grammar.apostrophes.01`

- Use an apostrophe plus s for singular possession, including singular names ending in s. Use an apostrophe after s for regular plural possession.
- Use its for possession and it's for it is or it has. Form ordinary plurals without apostrophes.
- Use a curly apostrophe in polished prose where supported. A straight apostrophe is acceptable in plain text and technical formats.

**Use:**

> The client's records are ready.

**Revise:**

> The clients records are ready.

This example refers to one client, so the possessive needs an apostrophe.

## 7. Quotation marks and faithful quotations

Rule: `aa.grammar.quotations.01`

- Use double quotation marks for direct quotations and single quotation marks for a quotation within a quotation or a term being discussed.
- Use curly quotation marks in polished publication copy where supported; straight marks are acceptable in plain text and technical formats.
- Place punctuation inside the closing mark when it belongs to the quoted material; otherwise place it outside. Preserve the quoted wording and meaning.
- Identify omissions or editorial additions transparently. Apply the canonical source-attribution and evidence requirements in the TOV policy.

**Use:**

> She described the process as “ready for review”.

**Revise:**

> She described the process as “ready for review.”

The full stop belongs to the surrounding sentence rather than the quoted phrase.

Exceptions:

- A required publication style can override quotation-mark typography; preserve quotation accuracy.

## 8. Dashes and hyphens

Rule: `aa.grammar.dashes_hyphens.01`

- Prefer a full stop, comma or colon when it expresses the relationship clearly. Use a spaced en dash for an occasional aside in prose.
- Use hyphens where they clarify a compound modifier before a noun. Do not add a hyphen after an adverb ending in ly.
- Write ranges with to in ordinary prose. An unspaced en dash is acceptable in compact tables and charts.
- Avoid unnecessary compound labels. Use familiar terms and preserve the spelling of established technical names.

**Use:**

> The agent uses real-time data and updates the record in real time.

**Revise:**

> The agent uses real time data and updates the record in real-time.

The compound modifier before data takes a hyphen; the adverbial phrase does not.

## 9. Measured emphasis and exclamation marks

Rule: `aa.grammar.emphasis.01`

- Use punctuation to make the point clear and measured. Exclamation marks are not used in authored brand prose.
- Use a question mark for a genuine question. Questions and expressive devices are optional; give the reader a useful reason to engage.

**Use:**

> Ready to test one workflow?

**Revise:**

> Ready to transform everything!

Ask a useful question and keep emphasis measured.

Exceptions:

- Exclude faithful quotations, required source wording, code, exact interface labels, URLs and Markdown image syntax.
- Labelled examples of wording to revise are excluded from whole-document style counts. Evaluate them separately only when requested.

## 10. Lists and bullet punctuation

Rule: `aa.grammar.lists.01`

- Use bullets for parallel items and numbers when order matters. Keep the grammatical form consistent within a list.
- Start each bullet with a capital letter unless it begins with an identifier whose case must be preserved.
- End complete sentences with full stops. Omit final full stops from short labels and fragments. Avoid mixing forms within one list.
- Use a clear lead-in and avoid trailing commas or semicolons on ordinary bullet items.

**Use:**

> 1. Agree the scope.
> 2. Name the owner.

**Revise:**

> 1. Agree the scope,
> 2. Naming the owner;

Keep the steps parallel and punctuate complete instructions with full stops.

Exceptions:

- Preserve exact code, mathematical notation, quoted lists and structured data.

## 11. Numbers, precision and rounding

Rule: `aa.grammar.numbers.01`

- In ordinary prose, spell out one to nine and use numerals for 10 and above. Use numerals for measurements, percentages, dates, comparisons and technical data.
- Use a decimal point and grouping commas for values of 1,000 or more in reader-facing prose. Keep machine-readable data and identifiers in their required format.
- Preserve source values and meaningful precision. If rounding is useful, use the stated method or round to the nearest chosen precision and label the result as approximate.
- Do not round selectively to strengthen a claim. Do not round values used to decide whether a threshold has been met. Retain the required precision for that decision.

**Use:**

> Illustrative value: 64.6%. Rounded to the nearest whole percentage: approximately 65%.

**Revise:**

> Illustrative value: 64.6%. Rounded to the nearest whole percentage: 64%.

Use consistent rounding and make the approximation explicit.

Exceptions:

- A required reporting method, scientific convention or client data format takes precedence over the prose default.

## 12. Dates, times and time zones

Rule: `aa.grammar.dates_times.01`

- Write human-readable dates as 11 November 2026, with no ordinal suffix or comma. Short month names are acceptable in compact tables.
- Use the 24-hour clock for schedules, such as 14:30. Include a time zone when readers could interpret the time differently.
- For UK schedules, use Europe/London to determine the correct GMT or BST designation for the stated date. Use explicit dates when relative wording could become unclear.
- Write periods unambiguously, such as every two weeks or twice a month. Use ISO dates and timestamps in machine-readable metadata.

**Use:**

> 11 November 2026, 14:30 GMT

**Revise:**

> 11/12/26 at 2:30

State an unambiguous date, time and relevant zone.

Exceptions:

- A specified client locale may use another unambiguous format. Preserve exact source quotations and machine formats.

## 13. Currency, percentages and units

Rule: `aa.grammar.currency_units.01`

- Use the appropriate currency symbol, such as £1,200. In mixed-currency or international contexts, make the currency explicit, such as GBP 1,200.
- Use numerals with % and no intervening space. Use a space between a number and a unit, such as 250 ms or 5 kg, unless the unit convention specifies otherwise.
- Preserve unit symbols and case. Distinguish bits from bytes and decimal units such as GB from binary units such as GiB.
- State a comparison baseline and distinguish a percentage change from a change in percentage points when the distinction matters.

**Use:**

> Illustration: a rise from 20% to 25% is 5 percentage points.

**Revise:**

> Illustration: a rise from 20% to 25% is 5%.

Distinguish the change in percentage points from the relative percentage change.

## 14. Acronyms and technical terminology

Rule: `aa.grammar.terminology.01`

- Explain an unfamiliar abbreviation on first relevant use, then use it consistently. Familiar terms such as AI can stand alone when the audience understands them.
- Use an approved product or service name exactly. Confirm an unfamiliar brand acronym or expansion against supplied approved terminology before using it.
- Avoid inventing acronyms, repeating unnecessary expansions or substituting jargon for a useful explanation.

**Use:**

> The application programming interface (API) connects the order system.

**Revise:**

> The API leverages an iPaaS across the RAG ecosystem.

Explain unfamiliar terms for a general business audience.

## 15. Names, roles, pronouns and agreement

Rule: `aa.grammar.names_agreement.01`

- Write Advanced Analytica with its approved capitalisation. Use we, you and your naturally where they help; third-person company descriptions are permitted.
- Use singular agreement for the company and for a team considered as one unit. Make pronoun references clear and use respectful, inclusive wording; singular they is acceptable.
- Use lowercase for generic job descriptions. Preserve the official capitalisation of a formal title used with a name or in a role listing.
- Use I for a subject, me for an object and myself only when the sentence calls for a reflexive or emphatic form.

**Use:**

> Advanced Analytica works with your team.

**Revise:**

> Advanced Analytica work with your team.

Use singular agreement for the company.

Exceptions:

- Preserve official names and faithful quotations, including their original spelling and grammar.

## 16. Formatting and spacing

Rule: `aa.grammar.formatting_spacing.01`

- Use bold sparingly for a key point, label or action. Keep ordinary prose in sentence case and use capitals for official names and established acronyms.
- Reserve underlining for the presentation of links. Use italics sparingly for light emphasis or cited titles.
- Use one space between sentences. Do not add spaces before commas, full stops, colons or semicolons in English prose.
- Preserve indentation, alignment and spacing required by code, tables and structured data.

**Use:**

```text
Agree the scope. Then name the owner.
```

**Revise:**

```text
Agree the scope.  Then name the owner.
```

Use one space between prose sentences.

## 17. Links and source presentation

Rule: `aa.grammar.links_sources.01`

- Use descriptive link text that tells the reader what they will open. Keep the complete working destination in the link target.
- Give the reader enough source information to identify the evidence behind a material claim. Follow the canonical evidence and attribution rule in the TOV policy.
- Use a short visible web address only when the address itself is useful, such as a contact detail. Preserve complete URLs where the medium requires them.
- Do not silently rewrite quoted wording, titles or numbers to match the house style.

**Use:**

> [Read the workflow guide](https://example.com/workflow)

**Revise:**

> [Click here](https://example.com/workflow)

The label identifies the destination. The example.com URL is illustrative.

Exceptions:

- Exact URLs can be necessary in technical instructions, references, plain-text output and audit records.

## Shared TOV rules

These requirements are read directly from their canonical TOV rules when this guide is generated.

### Plain language, optional contractions and sentence-length guidance

Canonical rule: `aa.tov.assured.02`

- Lead with the main point. Aim for an average of no more than 16 words per sentence. Review individual sentences over 24 words and prose paragraphs over 4 sentences. These are inclusive guidance thresholds, not automatic failure limits. Longer wording is acceptable when it improves precision or understanding.
- Use plain, natural British English at a level suited to the audience. Use familiar words and define specialist terms when the audience needs them. Replace corporate filler with concrete meaning; retain technical terms when they add precision. Contractions are optional. Formal wording and expanded forms are acceptable when appropriate to the context.

### Active voice and clear responsibility

Canonical rule: `aa.tov.assured.03`

- Prefer active voice when it clarifies who acts. Use passive voice when the actor is unknown, irrelevant or less important than the event. Name the responsible person or team for commitments.

### Evidence, uncertainty and source attribution

Canonical rule: `aa.tov.assured.01`

- Make direct, specific claims you can support. Attribute external findings and state material assumptions or limits. Use may, might, could and other qualifications when they express genuine uncertainty or conditional outcomes. Avoid filler hedging, unsupported guarantees and unsubstantiated superlatives. A number alone is not evidence.

## Review and maintenance

Record the rule, scope, excerpt and reason for each finding. Treat uncertain interpretation or unavailable context as REVIEW. Use NOT_APPLICABLE with a reason for excluded content. Automated semantic results are recommendations; an editor confirms the outcome.

Use deterministic checks only after excluding code, exact source text, markup syntax and other protected spans. Keyword matches and parser guesses are review signals unless a scoped rule defines a clear violation.

This file specifies evaluation requirements; it does not implement a complete grammar checker. Structural validation and generated-guide checks do not establish that arbitrary copy complies with every semantic rule.

Use the approval roles and workflow in governance_policy.json. Editing a draft does not record policy approval. Record the approval and effective date before activating this standard.

The grammar standard owns mechanical house conventions. The TOV policy owns the tone principles and shared semantic rules. Resolve references to their source and record each issue once.
